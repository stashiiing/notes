- # Last Mover Advantage
- ## TL;DR
	- Being the first mover in a market confers only a small tactical advantage. Being the last mover, the company that ends the competitive history of a category by making all future challengers irrelevant, is the real strategic prize. Because most of the value in a technology business exists in cash flows ten or more years in the future, durability matters far more than current growth rates. Build to be the last company standing, not the first to arrive.
- ### Context
	- Silicon Valley culture celebrates first-mover advantage and growth rates. Thiel argues this focus is misplaced. First movers can be displaced. Growth that does not compound into a defensible position is worthless. The correct question when evaluating any business is whether it will still be the dominant company in its category in ten to fifteen years.
- ## Core Concept
	- In chess, white's first-move advantage is roughly one-third of a pawn: meaningful but not decisive. The player who wins is the one who controls the endgame. The same logic applies in business. Microsoft was the last operating system for decades. Google is the last search engine. Facebook will be valuable if it proves to be the last social network. The last mover builds a position that makes the competitive history of the category irrelevant.
- ## Mechanics
	- Discounted cash flow analysis of any high-growth technology company reveals that three-quarters to 85 percent of total value comes from cash flows in years ten or more into the future. This means the growth rate you can measure today is far less important than the durability of the business's competitive position over the next decade.
	- The practical implication: prioritise building structural features that compound over time rather than optimising for near-term growth metrics. Ask not "how fast are we growing?" but "why will we still be the leading company in this category in fifteen years?"
	- Thiel verified this at PayPal in March 2001, 27 months into the business. With 100 percent annual growth and a 30 percent discount rate, approximately three-quarters of the company's value in 2001 came from cash flows in 2011 and beyond.
- ## Caveats & Edge Cases
	- This framework does not mean growth is unimportant. Fast adoption is often necessary to close a market before competitors can enter. The point is that growth without durability produces very little long-term value. A business that grows quickly and then loses its position is worth far less than one that grows more slowly but maintains dominance for decades.
	- Durability is harder to measure than growth, making it systematically underweighted in analysis. This is an exploitable blind spot.
