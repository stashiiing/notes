- # Market Definition Lies
- ## TL;DR
	- Both monopolists and non-monopolists distort how they describe their markets, but in opposite directions. Monopolists expand their stated market to look small within it; non-monopolists narrow their stated market to look like the only player. In set-theory terms, monopolists describe the union of vastly different markets while non-monopolists describe the intersection of narrow niches. Both moves obscure the truth and make the real competitive landscape harder to read.
- ### Context
	- When evaluating a business, the most important variable is market structure: is this company operating as a monopoly or in fierce competition? Both types of business have strong incentives to conceal the answer, so the stated market description is almost never reliable at face value.
- ## Core Concept
	- Non-monopolists shrink their market rhetorically to claim uniqueness they do not have. Monopolists expand theirs to claim competition they do not face. The real difference between these two situations is large; the apparent difference, shaped by these lies, is small. Cutting through the distortion requires reconstructing the actual competitive landscape from first principles rather than accepting the company's own framing.
- ## Mechanics
	- The non-monopolist move: define yourself as the intersection of multiple narrow categories to claim you are the only one. "The only British restaurant in Palo Alto" sounds unique but the relevant market is all restaurants within driving range, where competition is intense.
	- The monopolist move: describe yourself as a participant in a vast, competitive market by taking the union of adjacent categories. Google reframes itself from a search monopoly (approximately 66 percent market share) into a small player in online advertising, then in all US advertising, then in global advertising (500 billion dollars), then in the broader technology sector. Each expansion makes the dominant position disappear.
	- The tell for a non-monopolist framing: a rhetorical intersection of buzz-word categories that produces an impossibly specific niche. The tell for a monopolist framing: an ever-expanding market definition that always happens to show the company as a minor participant.
- ## Caveats & Edge Cases
	- Some genuinely new markets are correctly described as small and unique at inception. The test is whether the narrow definition reflects real structural isolation or is a rhetorical move to avoid acknowledging competition. Similarly, some large incumbent companies genuinely compete in broad markets rather than dominating narrow ones.
