- # Career Alpha via Risk Premia
- ## TL;DR
	- The same logic that explains risk premia in markets applies to careers. Undesirable activities eating rejection in sales, risking reputation in startups, looking foolish publicly carry enormous alpha precisely because most people, especially those with strong credentials and options, refuse to do them. The return is disproportionate to the actual difficulty. Positioning yourself on a growing macro curve amplifies the odds further; luck in which specific company you land at is substantial, so the sector matters more than the specific bet.
- ### Context
	- Many people who intellectually understand risk premia in finance never apply the concept to their own career decisions. The "risk" of looking like an idiot or failing publicly is often not a real financial risk at all it is a social and psychological one. Yet the returns associated with tolerating that discomfort are outsized because the pool of people willing to do it is thin.
- ## Core Concept
	- Two independent principles operating together: (1) collect the discomfort premium do the things others with your skill set refuse to do because it is unpleasant; (2) position on the right macro curve your sector matters more than your individual skill, because there is a large luck component in which specific company succeeds within a hot sector.
- ## Mechanics
	- **Collecting the discomfort premium:**
		- Sales is overcompensated relative to its actual cognitive difficulty because rejection is unpleasant and most people avoid it. The premium exists because of the psychology, not the complexity.
		- Fundraising for a startup or asset management firm is the same dynamic. People give money to people, not to strategies or return profiles. The willingness to endure repeated rejection is rare and therefore well rewarded.
		- Young people specifically have the highest risk-adjusted opportunity here: fewer lifestyle obligations, lower reputational cost of failure, less to lose.
	- **Macro curve positioning:**
		- Identify sectors with capital inflow, hiring momentum, and tailwinds.
		- Being in a hot sector raises both median outcomes and far-right tail outcomes simultaneously.
		- Within a hot sector, there is meaningful luck in which specific company becomes the winner; diversifying your bet at the sector level reduces dependence on that luck.
		- Entering a sector near the apex of its hype cycle (trillion-dollar IPO imminent) reduces the remaining risk-adjusted upside; timing matters.
		- Corollary: avoid sectors with no capital inflow you are pushing on a string regardless of skill level.
	- **On skill vs. passion:**
		- Passion follows competence more than competence follows passion. Focus first on what you are willing to spend time on, then build skill there. Skill identification precedes sector selection.
- ## Examples
	- Shane Coplan (Polymarket founder): had nothing to lose as a young founder, correctly identified that the regulatory workaround (USDC + VPN) did not need to be a formal legal solution, and built to scale before regulators could act. The same playbook Uber and Airbnb used.
	- LDrogen on Estimize: started the company at 22-23, took risks he could not have taken later with more to lose.
	- Sales compensation: salespeople are paid well relative to actual skill requirements specifically because eating rejection daily is psychologically costly and most people avoid it.
- ## Caveats & Edge Cases
	- Macro curve positioning can be overdone: hopping into a trend at its very peak (e.g. joining a company weeks before a trillion-dollar IPO with no remaining upside) may not be the right move.
	- The framework assumes the "discomfort" risk is psychological, not financial. Taking on real financial ruin risk is a different calculation, especially with dependants.
	- Luck in outcome is high even when all variables are optimised correctly; the framework improves odds, it does not guarantee outcomes.
